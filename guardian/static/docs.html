Start here

Parental Gateway sits between your home internet connection and every device in the house. Everything a phone, tablet, laptop or console sends to the internet passes through it, so it can filter what children can reach, show what they're doing, stop the usual ways of getting around a filter, and tell you when someone tries.

The three things that matter most
  1. Put your own devices in the "adults" group (Devices page). Everything else is filtered until you say otherwise.
  2. Turn off the Wi-Fi on your ISP's router and change its admin password. If children can join that Wi-Fi, they skip the gateway completely.
  3. Check the Health page now and then. It tells you if protection is working and who has been trying to get around it.

What it can and can't see

It can seeIt can't see
Which sites and services each device uses (youtube.com, roblox.com…)Which page or video on a site - pages are encrypted
How long and how much dataMessages, searches or passwords - they're encrypted
What a device is probably doing (gaming, video, downloading) from the shape of its trafficAnything done on mobile data or another Wi-Fi network
Attempts to get around the filter, and attacks on the gateway itselfTraffic between two devices inside your Wi-Fi (turn on "AP isolation" on your access point)

It never breaks encryption and never installs anything on children's devices. Everything it knows comes from the parts of internet traffic that are visible on any network.

How it works

The gateway is a small computer (a Raspberry Pi) wired between your ISP's router and your Wi-Fi access point. It becomes the router for your home: devices get their network settings from it, and it is the only way out to the internet.

The journey of one web request

Say a child types a web address. Here is what happens, in order:

  1. The device asks "where is example.com?" - a DNS lookup. The gateway answers it. If the site is in a blocked category, on a rule, looks adult by its name, or is flagged by the family DNS service, the answer is "doesn't exist" and the browser shows an error. For search engines, the answer points to the locked SafeSearch version. If the child had set their own DNS server, the question is quietly redirected to the gateway anyway.
  2. The device connects to the address it was given. The firewall only lets children's devices use normal web traffic (ports 80 and 443). VPN protocols, unusual ports, known VPN servers and encrypted-DNS servers are dropped here.
  3. The inspector reads the connection's opening - the first few packets, before anything is encrypted. It checks the site name the device is asking for, that it's a genuine web connection and not a disguised VPN, and that the address really belongs to a name the gateway handed out. Anything wrong: the connection is reset.
  4. The monitor watches the connection's traffic every 2 seconds: how much data, which direction, how steady. That tells it what the device is probably doing, notices downloads and unknown video, and records time spent.

Separately, the tamper watch listens to the home network for attacks on the gateway itself (pretending to be the gateway, copying a parent's device identity, rogue network settings).

Setting it up

What you need

  • A Raspberry Pi 4 or 5 (2 GB or more) with Raspberry Pi OS (64-bit).
  • A USB 3 gigabit Ethernet adapter - the Pi needs two network ports.
  • A Wi-Fi access point, or your existing Wi-Fi router switched to access point / bridge mode.

Wiring

ISP router → Pi's built-in Ethernet port. Pi's USB Ethernet adapter → Wi-Fi access point. Every device then connects to that access point.

Installing

On the Pi, from the project folder, run sudo ./deploy/setup-pi.sh. It lists the Pi's network ports and asks which one goes to the internet (your ISP router) and which to your home (the access point) - suggesting the likely answers. It then installs everything, gives the home side the address 192.168.50.1, hands out network settings to devices, starts the service and asks you for a dashboard password. Running it again later is safe: it updates the program and lets you re-pick the ports, and keeps your password, history and settings.

Opening the dashboard: http://family.local

Nobody needs to know the Pi's address. From any phone, tablet or computer at home, type family.local into the browser. The name is yours to choose in the setup wizard (for example smiths.local).

  • If a device can't open family.local (a few older Android phones), use family.home.arpa - it always works at home, because every device asks the gateway for addresses. The Pi's own address, 192.168.50.1, works too.
  • No port number needed - the Pi answers on the normal web port.
  • On a phone: System → Dashboard address shows a QR code - point the camera at it. Then use your browser's Add to Home Screen and the dashboard opens like an app, with its own icon.
  • Children's devices can look the name up, but only adults' devices can open the dashboard, and it always asks for the password.

How it works: the gateway's DNS answers the name with its own address, and on the Pi the name is also announced on the home network with mDNS (Avahi) - that's how iPhones, Macs and Windows find .local names. .home.arpa is the ending reserved for home networks, so browsers treat it as an address rather than a search.

The setup wizard

The first time you sign in to the dashboard, a short wizard walks you through the rest. Each step explains what its choices do:

  1. How it runs - the dashboard's name (what everyone types: family.local), and checks the machine is ready: the internet and home ports, the firewall, administrator rights, blocklists. On a PC it also asks which port DNS listens on and whether the dashboard can be opened from other devices.
  2. Protection level - Strict (recommended: suspicious downloads, unknown video and heavy traffic to unknown places are held for you; repeated bypass attempts pause the device), Balanced (the same blocking, but unknowns are reported rather than stopped) or Light (blocks, reports, never holds or pauses - for older teenagers).
  3. Fine-tune - every setting the level chose, individually.
  4. What to block - the site categories for children.
  5. Ways around - each anti-bypass protection (VPNs, Tor, proxies, encrypted DNS, disguised tunnels and the rest) can be switched on or off; each says what switching it off allows. All are on in every preset.
  6. Devices - name the devices seen so far and put each in a group. The device you're using is suggested as an adult's. You also choose what happens to new devices.
  7. Bedtime - an internet-off time for the children's groups (never adults).
  8. Privacy - how long history is kept, whether adults' activity is recorded, and (on the Pi) what happens if the inspector ever stops.

Most choices take effect the moment you press Finish. The few that can't change while the service runs (the DNS port and dashboard access on a PC, fail-open/closed on the Pi) make it restart itself - the page reconnects on its own after a few seconds, and you stay signed in. Choices are saved in config.local.yaml next to config.yaml. Run the wizard again any time from System → Run setup again. Which network port is which is deliberately not in the web wizard: one wrong click there would cut the dashboard off, so that stays in setup-pi.sh.

Then

  1. Set the access point to access-point / bridge mode (no DHCP of its own).
  2. Turn off the ISP router's Wi-Fi and change its admin password.
  3. If you skipped it in the wizard: move your devices to adults, consoles to consoles; leave children's devices where they are.
  4. Turn on client / AP isolation on the access point if it has the option (stops devices talking to each other directly).
  5. Keep the Pi and routers where children can't reach the cables.

On a Windows PC

The same program runs on Windows. In watch-only mode it shows what's happening and what would be blocked. Choose Block in the setup wizard (run it as administrator) and it protects this PC - every connection goes through the same checks as on the Pi, and its DNS lookups are filtered whatever server they were sent to - and devices on its Mobile Hotspot (turn on Settings > Network > Mobile hotspot). It uses the WinDivert packet filter driver.

  • Check it works: from an administrator command prompt, python run.py --windows-check tries what a child would (adult site, VPN server, Tor, WireGuard, encrypted DNS…) and reports what was blocked.
  • On a child's own PC: deploy\windows-install.ps1 makes it start with Windows and restart if it stops; give the child a standard (non-administrator) account so they can't stop it.
  • Unlike the Pi, it only covers this PC and its hotspot, and if the program isn't running, traffic flows unfiltered - which is why the Pi is the way to protect the whole house.

Updates

The gateway checks for a new version every hour, and System → Updates → Check for updates checks straight away. New versions install by themselves (or wait for you to press Install now, if you prefer - the switch is on the same panel). An update never interrupts the Wi-Fi or the filtering, not even for a second:

  1. Downloading - the new version is unpacked into a folder of its own. The running version isn't touched.
  2. Preparing - its Python packages are set up (reused if nothing changed; otherwise built in the background, which on a Pi can take a few minutes) and its code is pre-compiled so it starts quickly.
  3. Testing - it has to pass a self-test with your settings. A version that fails is thrown away and you're told why; nothing else happens.
  4. Switching over - the new version starts alongside the running one. It shares the DNS and dashboard ports, so lookups keep being answered by one or the other throughout. Once it's ready, the firewall is pointed at it in a single step - with every device list already filled in - and the old version finishes what it was doing and exits. Connections that were open carry on.

If the new version doesn't come up properly, the old one simply carries on. If it starts but then keeps crashing, the gateway goes back to the previous version by itself. You can also go back with Go back to version …, which is a seamless switch too. The last few versions are kept for this. Settings changes that need a restart (on the setup wizard's review page) use the same seamless switch.

Where updates come from is set under Where updates come from: a git repository (an https:// address), its branch, and the folder the program lives in. setup-pi.sh fills this in from the copy it was run from. Updates only install on a gateway set up with setup-pi.sh; a copy running from a development folder (e.g. on your PC) only checks, and is updated with git pull.

Groups

Every device belongs to one group, and the group decides its rules. New and unknown devices go into quarantine (as strict as kids) until you assign them, so a new phone, a friend's tablet or a device with a changed hardware address is filtered automatically.

Site filtering

Each group has a set of categories blocked. When a device looks up a site, the gateway checks:

  1. an allow rule (always wins - use it to unblock one site),
  2. a block rule,
  3. the group's category lists: ads, adult, gambling, VPN/proxy/bypass services, torrents, web mirrors, unlockable search engines.

The lists come from well-maintained public projects (Hagezi, StevenBlack) plus the gateway's own built-in lists, and refresh daily. A blocked site gets "doesn't exist" as its answer. The same checks run again on the site name inside each connection, so a site reached without asking the gateway (a hard-coded address, a hosts-file entry) is still caught.

The gateway also checks the alias chain of every answer: an innocent-looking name that quietly points to a blocked one is blocked too.

Adult content protection

A blocklist only knows sites someone has already listed. These layers go further, and close the indirect routes.

1. SafeSearch and YouTube Restricted Mode, locked on

When a child's device looks up Google, Bing, DuckDuckGo or Yandex, the gateway answers with that search engine's special "safe" address. The search engine then forces SafeSearch on, whatever the browser settings say - this is the mechanism the search engines themselves provide for schools and families. YouTube is answered with its Restricted Mode address, which hides mature videos and comments. Search engines that offer no way to lock SafeSearch (Brave Search, Startpage, Yahoo and others) are blocked so the locked ones are used instead.

2. A second opinion from a family DNS service

Children's lookups are answered through Cloudflare for Families, a free service that classifies millions of sites - including new ones no blocklist has caught up with - and flags adult and malicious sites. If it flags a site, the gateway blocks it even when its own lists don't know it. If the service can't be reached, lookups fall back to the normal resolver (and the counter on the System page shows it).

3. Reading the site name itself

Names containing strong adult terms (porn, xxx, hentai…) are blocked everywhere - in lookups and inside connections. In strict mode, weaker terms are blocked too, but only as whole words, so Essex, Middlesex or analytics are never caught.

4. Translators, caches and archives

A blocked site can be viewed through another service: Google Translate's page translator, Google's AMP page cache, web archives and "reader" sites. The page then comes from the third party's address, so the blocklist never sees the real site. The gateway handles this two ways:

  • Translator and AMP-cache addresses carry the real site in their name (www-example-com.translate.goog). The gateway decodes it and checks the real site against the lists, the name heuristics and the family DNS service. Ordinary translations still work.
  • Web archives and page caches (archive.ph, the Wayback Machine…) are blocked for children.

5. Video from sites nobody has listed

The monitor can tell video from other traffic by its shape (big bursts with pauses). In strict mode, video streaming from a site that isn't a known video service (YouTube, Netflix, Vimeo, BBC, Khan Academy… - editable) is cut and held for your review. This catches adult and pirate streaming sites that no list knows about yet. Approve once and that site is fine for that device from then on.

6. Everything else that already applies

VPNs, proxies, Tor, encrypted DNS, iCloud Private Relay, disguised tunnels and direct-IP connections are all blocked (see "Stopping workarounds") - those are the usual ways to reach blocked content "somewhere else".

What it can't do

  • Adult content inside allowed platforms. Reddit, X/Twitter, Discord, Telegram, Tumblr and similar host adult material behind encryption; the gateway sees "reddit.com", not which community. If that's a concern, block the platform for children with a rule, and use the app's own safety settings.
  • Images and video already on the device, or shared by message, AirDrop, Bluetooth or USB.
  • Mobile data. Use the phone's own controls (Screen Time / Family Link) as well.

Targeting the technology, not server lists

Lists of VPN servers and Tor relays help, but they can never be complete: there are hundreds of VPN services, they add servers every day, and anyone can rent a server of their own. Tor Browser can be copied in on a USB stick from a friend's house, so blocking its website changes nothing. So the gateway's main defences don't depend on knowing the server at all - they target how these tools work:

DefenceWhy no VPN or Tor can avoid it
Web ports onlyChildren's devices can only send normal web traffic. VPN protocols (WireGuard, OpenVPN, IPsec…), Tor's own relay port and most bridges simply can't leave the device - whichever server they're aimed at.
Genuine-protocol checkTraffic on web ports must really be a secure web connection or a web request. Disguised tunnels (Shadowsocks, obfs4 Tor bridges, "stealth" modes that fake it) fail.
Only addresses the gateway handed outVPN apps and Tor connect straight to server addresses they carry inside them; they never ask DNS. A child's device may only connect to addresses the gateway's own DNS gave it, so a brand-new server, an unlisted relay or a Tor Browser from a USB stick is refused on its very first connection.
Tor's handshake shapeTor's connections use made-up site names (www.random letters.com) that were never looked up - recognised and blocked even where the DNS check only reports.
Learned software fingerprintsEvery app opens secure connections with its own characteristic "hello" (its TLS fingerprint). When a tunnel is caught - by its behaviour, a hidden site name, a disguise, or your "Confirmed" click - its fingerprint is remembered, and that software is then blocked on any server, including ones that appear later. Fingerprints that browsers and ordinary apps use for normal sites are never learned. Learned fingerprints are listed on Rules & schedules.
Unknown destinationsThe one thing a tunnel with a genuine domain and genuine encryption (a VPN routed through a CDN, a Tor "WebTunnel" bridge, a VPN on a rented server) can't hide is that it carries a lot of traffic to somewhere. For children, heavy or long-running traffic to a site that isn't popular, a known service, used by an adult in the house, or approved by you is cut and held on the Reviews page. Hosting platforms where anyone can run a server (Cloudflare Workers, Amazon EC2, Heroku…) never count as known.
BehaviourA device whose DNS goes quiet while one long connection carries all its traffic is tunnelling. On unknown destinations this is caught in about 20 seconds.
EscalationA device that tries technique after technique within ten minutes is running a bypass tool: the alert names it (e.g. "Tor Browser") and the device is paused.

Example: Tor Browser brought in on a USB stick

  1. Tor Browser starts and tries to reach a relay by address on port 9001 or 443. Port 9001 can't leave the device; on 443 the address was never handed out by the gateway - refused. Relays that are publicly listed are dropped as well.
  2. It falls back to a bridge. obfs4 bridges send random-looking bytes - they fail the genuine-protocol check. Snowflake needs traffic children's devices can't send. Meek and similar need fronting the gateway checks for.
  3. A WebTunnel bridge uses a real domain and real encryption - its heavy traffic to an unknown destination is held, and its behaviour gives it away.
  4. After a few of these attempts the escalation alert names "Tor Browser (or another Tor-based app)" and pauses the device.

The same layers apply to any VPN app, whether it's on any list or not.

Torrents

Torrent clients download pieces of files from many strangers at once. The gateway recognises them by: torrent sites, trackers and the servers new clients use to find peers (blocked by name); the BitTorrent handshake, even when hidden on a web port; tracker announcements; the DHT and uTP protocols; a device trying to reach dozens of peers within a minute (catches fully encrypted clients); and torrent clients announcing themselves on your home network. Children's devices can't use the protocols torrents need anyway, so torrents are blocked several times over.

Protecting the gateway itself

A skilled child may attack the filter rather than avoid it. The tamper watch listens (without interfering) to the home network. Its one rule: automatic defences can take access away, never give it.

Download and video review

When a connection becomes a sustained one-way transfer, it's a download. Encrypted downloads can't be opened, so they're judged by where they come from and who's downloading: file-sharing, APK and cracked-software sites, torrent sites, sites first seen today, plain-HTTP executables, and whether this device has been trying to get around the filter. App stores, game platforms and system updates are trusted. Every point of the score is shown.

At 50 or more the download is cut and that site held for that device until you Approve (it can be retried) or Deny (the site stays blocked for that device). With plain HTTP the file name is visible, so a risky file (.exe, .apk, .zip…) is stopped before the first byte. Videos from unknown sites (see Adult content protection) appear here too.

Live activity analysis

Different activities move data in recognisably different ways, even when everything is encrypted:

ActivityLooks like
GamingSmall packets, a steady 10-60 a second in both directions, no pauses

DownloadingOne direction, flat out, no pauses

Each connection is classified every 2 seconds and the results combined: the interactive activity in front, video or music behind it. That's how "Likely playing an online game with YouTube in the background" comes about. It's an inference - the confidence and the reasons are always shown.

Confidence scores

Every alert says how likely it is to be a deliberate attempt rather than an app behaving oddly. It combines a base rate for that kind of event (splitting a connection into 1-byte pieces is almost never accidental; a connection by address is often just an app), the device's behaviour (several different tricks in a day raise every score) and your reviews: each Confirmed or Not a bypass recalibrates how much that kind of alert is trusted in your house. Hover over any score to see its reasoning and when it could be innocent.

Pauses and schedules

Pause a device for 15 minutes, an hour, or until you resume it. Schedules turn the internet off for a group or every device at set times (e.g. school nights 21:00-07:00), including windows that cross midnight. A paused device can't look anything up and its connections are refused straight away.

The technology, explained

Plain-English explanations of the technology involved - useful for understanding the alerts, and for talking to children about what they've tried.

Addresses, packets and ports

Every device and server on the internet has an IP address (like 142.250.180.14). Data travels in small packets, each labelled with where it's from and where it's going. A port number says which service it's for: 443 is secure websites, 80 plain websites, and other numbers are used by games, VPNs and so on. The gateway sees these labels on every packet, even when the contents are encrypted.

DNS - the internet's phone book

People use names (youtube.com); computers use numbers. Before connecting, a device asks a DNS server "what's the address for youtube.com?". Whoever answers that question controls where the device goes - which is why the gateway answers every DNS question itself, and why "use a different DNS server" is the first thing people try to get around a filter.

HTTPS, encryption and the site name

Almost every website now uses HTTPS: the connection is encrypted, so nobody in between can read the pages, searches or passwords. But the very first message of each connection - the "hello" - includes the name of the site being visited (called the SNI), unencrypted, so that big servers hosting many sites know which one is wanted. That's what lets the gateway know a connection is to youtube.com without being able to see which video. A newer feature called Encrypted Client Hello (ECH) can hide even that; the gateway prevents browsers from using it on children's devices.

VPNs - what they are, and how they're used to get round filters

A VPN (Virtual Private Network) creates an encrypted tunnel from a device to a VPN server somewhere else. Everything the device does online travels inside that tunnel and only comes out at the far end. To any network in between - including a home filter - all the device's traffic looks like one long encrypted connection to the VPN server. The filter can't see the sites inside, so it can't block them. That is why VPNs are the most popular way to "escape" a filter: install a free VPN app, tap Connect, and every block disappears - unless the network stops the tunnel from being set up.

The kinds children come across:

  • Free VPN apps from app stores (often funded by ads or by selling data). Usually WireGuard, OpenVPN or IPsec underneath - recognisable protocols the gateway blocks outright, and their login and server-list sites are blocked too.
  • Paid / privacy VPNs (Proton, Mullvad, NordVPN…). Same as above, plus published server lists the gateway downloads and blocks.
  • "Stealth" or "obfuscated" modes that disguise the tunnel as ordinary web traffic on port 443. Some fake a web connection (caught: not a real secure handshake). Some wrap the tunnel in genuine encryption (caught: the server's address was never handed out by the gateway, or it's on a VPN list, or its behaviour gives it away).
  • VPNs built into browsers (Opera, Brave, Microsoft Edge "Secure Network"). Their servers are blocked.
  • A VPN or proxy the child set up themselves on a cloud server or a friend's computer. Not on any list - which is exactly what the "addresses the gateway handed out" check, the unknown-destination hold, the behaviour detector and learned fingerprints are for.
  • VPNs routed through big CDNs (V2Ray/VLESS/Trojan on Cloudflare Workers and similar). They use a real domain and real encryption; the gateway treats hosting platforms as unknown, holds heavy traffic to them, and learns the client software's fingerprint once caught.

Signs a child has been trying: VPN and proxy alerts on the Alerts page, a device marked Curious or Persistent on the Health page, or a sudden "no internet" complaint from a device with a VPN app installed (the app is trying to connect and being refused).

Proxies and "unblocker" websites

A proxy fetches a page on someone's behalf. Web proxy sites ("unblock any website") let you type a blocked address into their page and show you the result; the network only sees the proxy site. Translators, page caches and web archives can be used the same way without meaning to be proxies. The gateway blocks proxy sites by category and handles the others as described in Adult content protection.

Tor

Tor sends traffic through three volunteer-run relays around the world, each only knowing the next step. It's used for privacy, and also to reach anything a network blocks. Tor Browser is a single program that can be copied onto a USB stick, so blocking its download page achieves little. What can't change is how it connects: straight to relay addresses it carries with it (never looked up in DNS - refused), using made-up site names in its handshakes (recognised), on Tor's own ports (blocked), or through "bridges" that either disguise themselves (rejected by the genuine-protocol check), need traffic children's devices can't send, or behave like a tunnel to an unknown destination (held). Public relay addresses are also blocked, and alerts name Tor explicitly. See "Targeting the technology, not server lists".

Encrypted DNS and private relays

DNS over HTTPS (DoH) and DNS over TLS (DoT, Android's "Private DNS") send DNS questions encrypted to a public server, so the home network can't answer or see them - which would skip every name-based block. The gateway blocks those servers and the apps fall back to normal DNS. iCloud Private Relay (Apple) is a VPN-like relay for Safari; Apple provides a way for networks to switch it off, which the gateway uses.

Mobile data, hotspots and other networks

A phone on 4G/5G, or connected to a friend's hotspot or a neighbour's Wi-Fi, isn't on your network at all. No home filter can see that traffic. Use the phone's own parental controls (Apple Screen Time, Google Family Link), mobile network content filters, and agreements about where devices are used.

MAC addresses, DHCP and ARP

Each network adapter has a MAC address - an identity used on the local network. The gateway attaches each device's group to it. When a device joins, it asks for its network settings using DHCP; the exact list of things it asks for differs between operating systems (its fingerprint). ARP is how devices on the same network find each other's MAC addresses; ARP spoofing is lying about it to intercept other devices' traffic. MAC addresses can be changed in software, which is why the gateway also uses fingerprints and treats any unknown identity as filtered.

Torrents and peer-to-peer

A torrent downloads a file in pieces from many other users ("peers") at the same time, while uploading pieces to others. It's widely used for pirated films, games and software - which often carry malware - and the uploading can expose a household to copyright notices. See Torrents for how they're recognised.

Deep packet inspection, and what encryption hides

Deep packet inspection means looking beyond a packet's address labels into its contents. With encryption, the only readable contents are each connection's opening messages - so the gateway inspects exactly those (the site name, whether it's a genuine secure connection, which protocol it really is) and then watches the shape of the rest (sizes, timing, direction). It deliberately does not decrypt anything: that would require installing a special certificate on every device, break many apps, and expose children's passwords and messages.

Incognito / private browsing

Private browsing only stops the device remembering history. It doesn't hide anything from the network - the gateway sees and filters private-browsing traffic exactly like normal browsing.

What it can't do, and what to pair it with

  • Mobile data and other networks - use device controls (Screen Time / Family Link) as well.
  • Content inside allowed apps and platforms - it sees reddit.com, not which community. Use apps' own safety settings, or block the platform.
  • Page-level history - it shows sites and services, not individual pages or searches.
  • Device-to-device traffic inside your Wi-Fi - turn on AP / client isolation on the access point.
  • Physical access - keep the Pi and routers out of reach, and the ISP router's Wi-Fi off.
  • Inferences can be wrong - activity descriptions and alerts are best judgements with their reasoning shown.

Technical controls work best alongside clear, agreed rules. Many families find that being open about the filter - and talking through an alert rather than just punishing it - leads to fewer attempts.

Troubleshooting & FAQ

Getting help: the diagnostics file

If something isn't working, go to System → Help & diagnostics, say in a sentence what's wrong, and press Download diagnostics. Send the file to whoever helps you with Parental Gateway. It contains the gateway's own logs (kept on the gateway, rotated so they never fill it up) and a snapshot of its state: version, which parts are running, the setup checklist, settings, update history, firewall rules, database health and recent alerts.

  • Private by default. Device names, MAC addresses and home-network addresses are replaced with labels like device-3; the dashboard password and sign-in sessions are never included. Sites visited are left out unless you tick the box for them.
  • If the dashboard won't open, the same file can be made on the Raspberry Pi with sudo parental-gateway-diagnostics, or on a PC with python run.py --diagnostics.
A site that should be fine is blocked.
Find it on the Activity page (Lookups, "blocked only") to see why, then add an allow rule on Rules & schedules for the group or everyone.
An online game doesn't work.
Most games need UDP traffic. Put the console or gaming PC in the consoles group - VPN and torrent protocols stay blocked there.
An app keeps failing with "no internet".
Check Alerts for that device. If it's an app with built-in server addresses (some messaging apps), click Not a bypass on the alert to let its destination through.
A school or news site's video was held.
Approve it on the Reviews page - that site is then fine for that device. Add sites you trust everywhere to adult.video_sites in the config.
My own device shows "identity check".
Its network fingerprint changed (an OS update or new adapter). Click It's me on the device page.
I'm locked out of the dashboard.
On the Pi: sudo /opt/parental-gateway/.venv/bin/python run.py --set-password. Only devices in the adults group can reach the dashboard once at least one is assigned.
The gateway stopped - is the internet down for everyone?
Briefly, yes. The firewall stays in place, so adults' open connections carry on and children's new web connections are refused (it fails closed). But the gateway also answers everyone's DNS lookups, so new lookups fail for every device until the service is back - systemd restarts it automatically within a few seconds.
Will this slow the internet?
Not noticeably. Only the first two or three packets of each connection are inspected; after that the connection skips the filter and, on a Pi, is forwarded by the kernel's fast path. DNS answers are cached, so most lookups are faster than without it. In tests a child's downloads ran at the same speed as an adult's, and opening a connection took under a millisecond longer.

Privacy and data

Everything stays on the gateway - nothing is sent to any outside service except DNS questions (to Cloudflare, and Cloudflare for Families for children's devices) and the daily blocklist downloads. It stores lookups, connections (site, timing, data volume), activity summaries and alerts for 30 days by default; no page contents, messages or passwords are ever seen. Adults' devices are not filtered or inspected, though their sites and data use are recorded unless flows.log_trusted_devices is turned off.
